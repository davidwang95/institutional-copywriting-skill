# Format guidance

Adapt to the user's template and purpose. These are choices, not mandatory layouts.

## Presentation copy

A takeaway title makes a supported point. A subtitle can identify the period, population, or lens. Put the evidence in a chart, table, or a few concise bullets. Keep sources and methodological limitations readable. A section label identifies the topic; it need not make a claim.

Use subject, change or gap, and a material period or benchmark where needed. Across slides, make the headlines form a coherent argument. Do not require a word limit, a conclusion banner, or an implication on every slide.

Arrange bullets so their sequence builds the argument. Start parallel bullets with comparable grammatical forms, but do not compress different meanings into artificial symmetry. A bullet should usually express one claim; keep the condition attached if splitting would overstate it. Avoid long chains of semicolons that conceal a paragraph.

Separate measured results, proposed explanations, and implications. If a chart shows association, the title cannot assert a causal mechanism. If a figure is a management target, label it as a target wherever a reader could mistake it for an outcome. Chart headings, legends, axis units, time periods, definitions, and notes are part of the claim.

Label metric, unit, period, population, and comparison basis. Preserve adjusted-metric exclusions and peer-selection limits. Distinguish percentage change, percentage-point change, and basis points; show the arithmetic for any requested derived value. Do not repeat chart labels as bullets unless the repetition explains the finding.

## Executive summaries

Lead with the supported assessment and the decision it informs. Follow with the most material evidence and qualifications. Keep adverse evidence visible. State a recommended action only if the assignment calls for one and the evidence supports it. A summary may omit detail, but it must retain limitations that would change the reader's assessment.

## Research notes and committee memos

Organize around the question, assessment, evidence, drivers, and risks where useful. Distinguish the base case from scenarios and from observed results. Explain assumptions that control the conclusion. Use exhibits to carry detailed evidence without making the prose repeat every data point.

An implication describes what the evidence means for the question. A recommendation proposes an action. Keep the distinction clear. If a sensitivity, threshold, or monitoring indicator would clarify the argument and is supported, state it precisely; do not manufacture a catalyst or threshold to complete the structure.

## Sources and footnotes

Keep the source attached to the relevant evidence. Preserve whether data is reported, modeled, estimated, surveyed, or otherwise incomplete. A nearby note can carry detail, but it cannot repair an unconditional headline that readers see first. Never fabricate page numbers, source links, research dates, or institutional attribution.
